# What the rules cover

add to this card's world book ("orcs in London since the 900s"); the model drafts the entry, you edit and approve it. `/world-without Karl Benz, 1880` removes a person from history from that year: each of their inventions is made by another inventor, made later, or never made, drawn from the published rates of independent invention. `/world-event The plague reaches Cambridge; Isaac Newton; 1665` removes the named people from that year. `/world-earlier telephone, 1850` makes an invention come early, if a first inventor that far ahead of the field is drawn. A character cannot claim, buy or find a thing not yet invented at the story's date; things named in your message are linked to Wikidata items ("Read as: minigun → M134 Minigun (1960)"), and you pick when two fit equally. Items keep an owner apart from who holds them (lent, stolen). Speech in another language can be misunderstood where that language uses one word for two meanings (CLICS⁴); the misreading shows later, through what the listener does. A character without a voice whispers; words that differ only in voicing (big/pig) are misheard at the measured rates. Whispers carry about 2 m in a quiet room. A listener follows a related language at the share measured in people who had never heard it before (a Portuguese listener follows 62 % of Spanish, a Dane 44 % of Swedish). The narrator sees what the characters in the scene know, not what absent characters know; a fact can be marked secret. Misheard words are shown as they sound (sètï / sèdï). In noise, words that differ in one consonant are misheard at the rates Miller & Nicely measured. Languages follow the story's year: "japanese" in 1200 is Late Middle Japanese, and modern Japanese in 1200 is refused unless the world book has a time traveller. A thing a country had not adopted yet (a telephone in a country before its first line, from the CHAT dataset) can be claimed only with a believable path. Characters forget facts at measured rates by their age when they learned them; studied knowledge fades to about half and then holds. Whether a character can read, count, speak a language, hold a faith or has lived to an age starts from the measured share of their country's people in that year (literacy, numeracy, where each language is spoken); heights before 1896 and labourers' wages outside Europe (1820–2008) come from Clio Infra. Prices cover about 160 places 1209–2008 (a day's wage in subsistence baskets from Moatsos & de Zwart; the hourly pay of a named trade, 1953–2008, from Occupational Wages around the World); a place's population, share in towns, murder rate and infant mortality come from Clio Infra, and a city's size from Modelski, Chandler and Reba et al. For a real place on a date from 1940 to last week, the weather is the weather that was recorded that day (ERA5). A claim that the character once did something ("I've hit someone before") starts from the share of people of their sex who had ever done it, among 600 measured acts (36 % of men had hit someone who annoyed them), moved by their personality. A character who acts against their personality (a calm man screaming in church) is checked against how often people at their trait level act that way and how fitting the act is in that setting; a chain of reasons that make it fit raises the chance. A stranger in England around 1881 is drawn from that census; elsewhere their age and sex come from their country's population in the story year (in Brazil in 1990, a third are children), and from 1969 whether they work and in what kind of job come from their country's labour surveys, and from 1960 how many people they live with and how related. A cut leg or arm artery runs on a full physiology simulation (Pulse): the pulse climbs and the pressure falls as blood is lost, and the bleeding slows as the pressure drops. Sleep debt builds over short nights (14 nights of 6 h perform like one night without sleep), thirst shows at 2 % water loss, bleeding shows its pulse and breathing signs, walking pace follows slope, age, sex and the country's measured pace of life (31 countries: an Irish walker is 1.5 times as fast as a Brazilian) when the story does not set it, and Disease covers flu, colds, measles and the death tolls of historical plagues. `/world-list` shows the book. The panel has the same, with a remove button per entry.

. Whether a character has married starts from the share of people of their sex and age who were single in their country's census (1891–1952); refusing an unfair split, punishing a cheater and obeying an order to harm follow measured rates, obedience lower in modern years than in Milgram's. Drinking untreated water can infect a character, by the source (sewage, a fouled river, a well, rainwater) and the amount drunk, unless it was boiled. Weather in past years runs warmer or colder than today by the recorded climate of that month in that country (Berkeley Earth, 1750–2020), and a new character in 15 European and Central Asian countries gets an eye and hair colour from measured shares. From 1851 to 1939 a real place gets that day's reconstructed weather (20th Century Reanalysis), when SillyTavern's CORS proxy is on. A drink raises blood alcohol by the drinker's own body water (sex, age, height, mass), tiredness depends on the hour as well as the hours awake, and going without food takes weight off. Facts met again and again last longer, a long list heard once is only partly kept, and a character may forget to do what they meant to. How much a stranger is trusted, whether a false detail told after an event replaces what was seen, and whether people of the scene's country condemn an act follow measured rates; new characters' traits lean by their sex and age. Homicide before 1800 comes from Europe's historical records, and divers see by how clear the water is. What a character chooses leans with their temperament, fear, anger and drink, and a body that cannot stand cannot fight or run. Jumping a ditch and lifting a load come from the body's measured jump and lifting norms by sex and age. Hours at a task and thirst make slips likelier; hunger and pain, measured to make no difference, do not. A group stating something wrong sways a character by its size, sex and era; a witness picks a face out of a lineup from how well they saw it and how long ago. The status names how people typically feel at the activity a character is at. Eating beyond need puts weight on, starving kills at the body's own lowest weight, and a crowd leaving through one door takes the time the crowd model gives, with people crushed when it panics. Fear makes a hard problem harder to work out; sadness, measured to make no difference, does not. A witness who was afraid, who saw a weapon, or who rarely meets people of the culprit's kind picks the culprit less often, and a lineup shown one face at a time draws fewer wrong picks. A crowd pressed long enough can kill by crushing the breath out of people. Someone who did a small favour is a little likelier to grant a bigger one, more so when someone else asks later; someone who just refused a large request says yes to a small one more often than they follow through. A drowning person pulled out comes through or not by how long they were under water. Children run at the speed measured at their age, a breath held on going under lasts longer in warm water than in cold, and a handgun shot hits by the distance and the size of the one shot at. A listener who knows the speaker spots lies a little better, an interrogator believes less, and someone with much to lose is believed less. A figure in grey against woods is hard to see and one in black on snow easy, and a long look sees more than a glance. Hot and cold rooms make slips likelier. A character's own ability is set against the students the problem norms came from, who were brighter than average by less in later decades. A drink takes most of an hour to reach its peak, sooner and lower after a meal, and who can swim depends on sex and country. A room emptying through one door is worked out four times faster and keeps one number per crowd in the turn's record (it kept millions), and a result names only the judged values that could change it. A maidservant or a waitress is always a woman and a potman a man, whatever the trade's census share. Whether a character makes someone out is decided by sight (light, distance, size) whatever verb of seeing the narrator uses: watch, observe, glimpse, discern, make out or espy as well as see and spot. The same holds for every other task with its own rule: a narrator who writes eavesdrop, swam, cling, fled, fool, punch, stab, bandage, court or besiege gets hearing, swimming, holding on, the chase, the lie, the blow, the treatment, the meeting or the battle worked out, not a guess; and lying down, bolting a door or a shot of whisky are no longer taken for a lie, a missile or a gunshot. A player who writes "I glimpse" or "I club" has the look or the blow drafted as for "I look" or "I punch". After each message the player sees how it was read, in story words; when the words leave open who is struck or what "follow" means, the likeliest reading is taken and shown unless a wrong guess would hurt someone, and then the player is asked; "no, Will" in the next message redoes the last turn. The reading stays under the player's message, folded to one line of tagged calls (one colour per kind of rule, colour-blind safe and legible on every shipped theme), in a book serif of the story's era; a guess offers the other readings as links that fill the input box with the correction; questions are one sentence with their choices linked alike and numbered, with no default. How a line is said is picked from a toolbar of plain words over the selected words (pitch, loudness, pace, pauses, breath, voice quality, stress, feeling), kept unseen in the line, shown as soft underlines in the box and typeset in the chat; code reads it as conversation analysis's and VoQS's marks, sets the speech's loudness and feeling, and the listener hears the feeling right as often as listeners do (Juslin & Laukka 2003). The narrator, for its part, must name what each body task is. Taking a look, stealing a glance, throwing a punch, befriending or eluding someone are read as what they are, from FrameNet's frames as well as VerbNet's classes. The player's line is read by its grammar (the Cambridge Grammar of the English Language): "give Tom a kick" is a kick and "take a walk" a walk, while "give the guard a coin" hands a coin over and "give her a ring" is left to the scene; "I make Tom walk" and "I rely on Tom to keep watch" are Tom's acts; "I fail to climb", "I refrain from walking" and "I don't run" are no acts; "him" is the one man named before; "I am hit by Tom" is Tom's blow and "Tom is hit by me" mine; "I could hit him", "I hit him if he moves" and "Tom wants me to walk" are no acts; and a cup of tea has a price where a pack of wolves has none. A run the line refuses ("I don't run. I walk 10 m to the door.") is walked. Its time and its kind of clause are read too: "Today I walk to the gate" and "In the morning I punch Tom" are acts now, while "I walk to the market every day", "Yesterday I walked…", "I used to punch Tom" and "I am about to walk" are not; "Do I walk to the door?" asks and "Let's walk to the door" proposes; "Tom, punch the guard!" is speech to Tom; "I give the coin to Tom" and "I take the coin and give it to Tom" hand the coin over; "A man walks in. I punch him." strikes the man; "It is Tom that I hit" is a blow at Tom; and the acts are written in the order the line tells them. A walk ends where its words put it: "I walk 10 m towards the door" stops short of the door, "across the room" ends on its far side, "along the wall" somewhere along it, "there" at the place just named; and "I walk for an hour" is an hour spent walking. Before the grammar reads a line, it is read for what the player meant, as listeners read speech: a slip ("I punhc the gaurd") is the word the scene makes likeliest, "again" is the last act, "I hit him back" is a blow at whoever just struck, "him" is weighed by how speakers use pronouns for the one in focus (taken for a word, asked for a blow), and a goal the player stated ("I'm looking for the key") fills a later search. Each reading is shown under the line.
